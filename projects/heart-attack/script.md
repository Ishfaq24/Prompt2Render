## 1 - Coronary Anatomy & Plaque Formation
> ANIM: Macro cardiac anatomy showing the left anterior descending coronary artery branching over the myocardium; transitions into a cross-sectional view of the arterial lumen where a lipid-rich atherosclerotic plaque progressively narrows the vessel channel

The human heart beats over one hundred thousand times each day, powered by a dedicated vascular network known as the coronary arteries. The left anterior descending artery wraps around the myocardium, delivering oxygenated blood. Over decades, circulating low density lipoproteins penetrate the endothelial barrier, oxidizing and sparking chronic vascular inflammation. Macrophages engulf these lipids to become foam cells, building an atherosclerotic plaque with a lipid core beneath a collagen fibrous cap.

## 2 - Plaque Rupture & Acute Thrombosis
> ANIM: High-magnification arterial cross-section with camera zooming into the fibrous cap as hemodynamic stress causes mechanical fissure; tissue factor exposure triggers immediate platelet adhesion and a dense fibrin thrombus, completely occluding the blood lumen

Under elevated hemodynamic sheer stress, the fragile fibrous cap suddenly tears open. The exposed thrombogenic core releases tissue factor directly into the luminal bloodstream, triggering explosive platelet activation and a fibrin clotting cascade. Within seconds, a dense thrombus consolidates across the vessel, causing acute ninety-nine percent luminal occlusion. Arterial blood flow halts abruptly, starving downstream myocardial cells of vital oxygen and glucose.

## 3 - Ischemia, Necrosis, and STEMI
> ANIM: Overview of the ischemic myocardial zone shifting from vital red to necrotic purple; a real-time electrocardiogram trace displays progressive ST-segment elevation as cellular ATP depletes and coagulative necrosis begins

Deprived of perfusion, cardiac myocytes switch to anaerobic metabolism, rapidly exhausting cellular adenosine triphosphate. Transmembrane ion gradients collapse, causing cellular swelling and generating injury currents that produce the classic ST-segment elevation on an electrocardiogram. Without immediate reperfusion, irreversible coagulative necrosis takes hold within twenty minutes, resulting in permanent infarction of the heart muscle.
